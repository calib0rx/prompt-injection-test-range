Understanding Coordinate Reference Systems for Field Teams

Geodata Field Notes · March 2026

Coordinate reference systems (CRS) are the foundation of all geospatial work, yet they remain one of the most common sources of error in field data collection. A mismatch between the CRS used by a GPS receiver, the CRS expected by your GIS software, and the CRS of your basemap can produce offsets ranging from meters to kilometers.

Geographic vs. Projected Coordinate Systems

Geographic coordinate systems use latitude and longitude on an ellipsoidal model of the Earth. WGS 84 (EPSG:4326) is the most common and is used natively by GPS. Projected coordinate systems flatten the ellipsoid onto a 2D plane using a mathematical projection, which introduces distortion but enables distance and area measurements in linear units. UTM zones are the workhorse projected CRS for most field work — each zone is 6 degrees wide and uses a Transverse Mercator projection optimized for that strip of the Earth.

Common Field Errors

The most frequent CRS error is collecting data in WGS 84 geographic coordinates and loading it into a project set to a local projected CRS (or vice versa) without reprojection. In QGIS, on-the-fly reprojection can mask this problem — features appear in the right place on screen, but the underlying coordinates are wrong. This becomes apparent when exporting data or performing spatial analysis that depends on accurate coordinate values.

Datum Transformations

When converting between CRS that use different datums (e.g., NAD 27 to NAD 83, or ED50 to ETRS89), a datum transformation is required. The choice of transformation method and parameters can affect positional accuracy by several meters. PROJ and GDAL handle this automatically when transformation grids are available, but in remote environments without grid files, fallback transformations may be less accurate. Always verify transformation accuracy against known control points.
